# Fictional examples

These examples illustrate how to use the skill. They are not records of any actual conversation or coaching outcome.

## Test the concern before reassuring the team

**Draft:** “We had a strong month. I think we’re fine.”

**Context:** A teammate asked whether the new approach will pay off after an unusually strong launch. They haven't said which part of the approach worries them.

**Feedback:** The result is relevant, but it doesn't answer whether the work pays off repeatedly. Starting with reassurance could close the conversation before you understand which part is in question.

**Suggested wording:** “Which part feels like it won’t pay off again? The launch went well, but I want to separate what we can repeat from what was a one-off.”

## Give an open question enough shape

**Draft:** “What should we do about onboarding?”

**Context:** Some new users create an account but never start the first task. The team is deciding what to test next.

**Feedback:** Keep the question open, but give the team your read and a concrete alternative to react to. Avoid treating the untested cause as a fact.

**Suggested wording:** “My read is that people are getting through signup and getting stuck at the first task. I’d test a prefilled example before we rewrite the whole flow. What are you seeing that might point somewhere else?”

## Recognize a good repair

**Sent:** “I said we should wait. To clarify: keep the existing work moving. The pause only applies to new requests until we see the revised plan.”

**Feedback:** The follow-up names both what continues and what stops. If the surrounding thread confirms people understood the clarification, say so. No rewrite is needed.

## Leave a clear message alone

**Draft:** “I recommend a small onboarding test before we rebuild the flow. We don't yet know why people stop at the first task. I’ll bring two test options to our next discussion; please flag any constraint I’m missing.”

**Context:** The writer is asking for input on a proposed experiment and owns the next step. No additional deadline has been agreed.

**Feedback:** No meaningful issue stands out in the supplied context. The message gives a recommendation, states uncertainty, invites constraints, and names the next owner. No rewrite or invented deadline is needed.

## Don't attribute an uncertain meeting comment

**Source:** A meeting summary says, “The team decided to keep the current approach.” It does not identify who said what.

**Request:** “Did I dismiss the concern too quickly?”

**Feedback:** The summary doesn't establish what you said or how you responded to the concern. I can't assess that from this source. A speaker-attributed excerpt or your own account of the exchange would help; I shouldn't turn the summary into a quote from you.

## Onboard on a host with an hourly limit

**Request:** “Connect my work chat and meeting notes and set up feedback every 30 minutes.”

**Environment:** The host can read the user's authorized Slack scope and meeting-notes folder, but its scheduler supports hourly runs at most.

**Setup behavior:** Confirm the source scope and private destination, verify access, and explain that 30-minute scheduling is unavailable. Offer hourly reviews and wait for acceptance before creating that schedule. Do not create two staggered hourly tasks or claim 30-minute coverage.

## Catch a late transcript without repeating feedback

**Situation:** A meeting transcript becomes available two hours after the meeting. The next scheduled review finds it. A Slack thread already reviewed has also gained a clarifying reply from the user.

**Review behavior:** Treat the newly available transcript as new material even though the meeting happened earlier. Verify the speaker before coaching. Read the Slack clarification and recognize any repair rather than repeating the original criticism. Send only useful new feedback; retain source checkpoints if retrieval fails.
